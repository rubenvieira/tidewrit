# Grain-scale rendering

The simulation surface remains the existing 512-square heightfield mesh. The realism pass deliberately keeps the deployed rendering architecture and its interaction/performance profile: there is still one bounded 3×3 procedural-grain neighborhood search per visible surface fragment, no explicit bed-grain mesh, no per-pixel raymarch, and no additional render pass.

The former grain field used a regular 0.43 mm jittered Voronoi lattice. At a glance it was convincing, but close inspection exposed the repeated statistical structure: grain footprints were too similar, dark minerals were distributed too uniformly, and the micro-relief read more like a material function than irregular packed beach sand.

The revised field keeps the same neighborhood count but makes each candidate grain anisotropic. The existing per-cell random values now determine an orientation and aspect ratio, and distances are evaluated in that grain-local metric. A small smooth world-locked coordinate warp bends the otherwise regular lattice without introducing another stochastic texture lookup. The winning grain therefore has a distinct elongated/rounded footprint while the shader still evaluates the same nine neighboring cells as before.

The selected grain also receives a second deterministic seed after the neighborhood search. That seed drives crown height, faceting, mineral family, roughness, and restrained translucency. Because the extra seed is evaluated only for the winning grain rather than for every candidate, the realism work stays bounded.

Material response now uses overlapping quartz-like, feldspar-like, pale mineral, iron-rich, and dark heavy-mineral families. A very coarse world-space composition seed only biases which families appear in a region; it does not directly multiply the surface color, so composition can drift over centimeters without introducing visible patch boundaries. The overall sand palette, direct-light level, environment model, and existing post-processing path are intentionally preserved from the checkpoint.

Grain-scale depth remains a small fragment-depth displacement derived from the selected analytic crown. Micro-normal direction follows the gradient of the anisotropic grain metric, including both aspect transforms from the chain rule, rather than a circular radial bump. The resulting facet perturbation is projected into the macro surface's tangent plane, so a tilted or nearly vertical face retains its geometric orientation instead of gaining a spurious normal-direction component. Crevices retain stronger local occlusion, while translucent mineral families receive only a restrained body-light term. This is intentionally a small optical correction rather than a full subsurface or path-traced transport model.

The same wet-film and kinetic-packing attenuation now scales both crown depth and its normal perturbation. A flooded or compressed surface therefore loses optical relief consistently; smoothing only the normal left full dry-grain depth protruding through an apparently smooth surface and occluding landing grains. The mineral palette, projected grain filtering, and existing residual relief under a film are preserved. Numerical GPU tests check the ellipse gradient against finite differences, tangent-plane orientation on steep faces, and the attenuation response.

The key performance constraint is that this pass does **not** double the grain candidate population. The abandoned heavy approach evaluated eighteen analytic candidates per fragment with multiple per-candidate trigonometric/power operations and replaced the display pipeline at the same time; at multi-million-pixel drawing buffers that could saturate the GPU badly enough that simulation/input appeared non-responsive. The shipped retry instead keeps nine candidates, computes the two smooth warp waves once per fragment, and leaves the established HDR-to-display path untouched.

## Mobile alias control

The mobile artifact was isolated to the procedural grain material itself, not the simulation grid, lighting cache, or mobile layout. `grainAppearance()` places one randomly jittered grain inside every regular 0.43 mm world-space cell. In a portrait phone view, perspective drives that cell spacing through roughly one rendered pixel across the middle of the bed. Sampling the regular cell lattice at the regular framebuffer lattice creates the visible fan/ring moiré pattern. A standalone reproduction of only the grain function and camera projection produces the same pattern, while the heightfield normal signal does not produce that fan structure.

Desktop keeps the original shader unchanged. Mobile keeps the same world-space grain function as well, but once the projected grain spacing enters the alias-prone range, each framebuffer pixel takes a stable stochastic sample at a different location inside its actual world-space pixel footprint. This breaks the coherent phase relationship between the two lattices and turns the unresolved sub-pixel detail into the grain's own stochastic statistics instead of a large-scale false pattern. The stochastic sample is already the anti-moire filter, so mobile does not additionally fade visible grain albedo, micro-normal, roughness, or crevice contrast as the footprint grows; doing both created a strong top-to-bottom sharpness gradient in portrait views. Fragment-depth grain relief is still independently faded once it is too small to resolve, which avoids noisy sub-pixel depth without softening the visible material. Desktop remains unchanged.

## Sun glints and bloom

Reflective mineral chips stay physically tiny and sparse. Candidate chips are world-locked in the existing 4.8 mm cells, with physical radii of roughly 0.06-0.14 mm. Their visible sparkle is not produced by enlarging the mineral itself.

For each candidate, the shader computes the microfacet slope required to reflect the real sun direction into the fixed camera. The glint now uses the resolved grain micro-normal and a material-family affinity, so dark/rough grains are less likely to produce the same flash response as pale reflective minerals. Rather than testing a handful of explicit facet normals, it evaluates a broad crystal-slope distribution at that required slope and makes one deterministic Bernoulli draw per chip. This is a bounded stochastic-microfacet approximation: the chip represents many unresolved crystal faces, and only a small stable subset produce a solar flash. Changing the surface normal or light geometry changes that probability naturally.

The physical chip remains sub-pixel, but its solar reflection receives a minimum approximately one-pixel optical footprint so its energy cannot disappear between framebuffer samples. The final visible sparkle shape is intentionally completed in post as a soft optical event with a compact core, short streaks, and a smeared glow rather than as a simple white disk. Coconut/direct-light visibility attenuates the glint before it is written to the second MRT attachment (`r16float`). The post composite additionally scales the visible sparkle footprint and gain down in portrait mobile viewports so the flash stays visually proportional to the finer apparent grain scale there. The base sand radiance is unchanged.


## Steep faces and natural variation

Grain coordinates are triplanar. The single projection from above stretched grains into vertical streaks on castle walls, cut faces and hole sides, so pixels on steep faces (macro normal y below about 0.58) take their grain from the side plane facing the dominant horizontal normal axis. Through the transition band, each pixel picks one plane by a stable screen-space dither on steepness rather than evaluating and blending two grain fields. That keeps the 3×3 grain search single per fragment and avoids ghosting two grain patterns over each other; on sand the dither is invisible. Screen derivatives for all three planes are taken in the fragment entry, in uniform control flow, so mip-free filtering and the phone stochastic sampling work for whichever plane is chosen.

Steep faces also get a little millimetre-scale relief from a few incommensurate plane waves in 3D (a cheap smooth gradient field), projected into the surface's tangent plane and faded in by steepness. Without it, molded walls and cut faces read as glassy; the relief is reduced on packed kinetic sand and under a standing water film, which are genuinely smooth. The six cosine waves are evaluated only when their final weight is nonzero, skipping this work on flat/shallow sand and fully flooded faces without changing their shading. The relief feeds diffuse and ambient shading only. Specular, sky reflection and glints use the grain-level normal without it, because the relief stands in for unresolved roughness at every scale: as a smooth wave field it formed coherent facets that caught a bright, plastic-looking sun reflection on a low-sun spoil heap.

Beach sand albedo carries faint broad tone swells (±9% and ±5% at 11 cm and 3.7 cm) and patches where dark heavy minerals have collected. These are world-locked, so they stay put when sand is moved, the same as the procedural grains themselves; they are kept low-contrast for that reason. Kinetic sand is uniform and skips them.
