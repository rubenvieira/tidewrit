# Bed-space horizon lighting

Macro illumination runs on the simulation grid before drawing, rather than repeating a heightfield raymarch at every screen pixel. The surface bilinearly reconstructs direct visibility and sky visibility; submillimeter grain normals, minerals, and specular response remain per fragment. The existing direct-light sample positions and penumbra approximation are retained. This trades screen-space shadow evaluation for grid-scale interpolation, not additional geometric detail.

Sky occlusion takes the maximum horizon in eight directions over six distances (one through 32 grid cells). Horizons are measured above the local tangent, preventing a uniformly tilted plane from being treated as a cavity. Squared horizon sines approximate cosine-weighted missing sky. This is a bounded heightfield approximation, not full global illumination. No temporal noise or accumulation is used, so stationary lighting does not shimmer or trail a stroke.

The horizon search compares slopes directly because their angles have the same ordering. For tangent slope `t` and highest slope `h ≥ t`, the squared missing-sky sine is `(h − t)² / ((1 + h²)(1 + t²))`; when `1 + h·t ≤ 0`, the horizon is at least 90° above the tangent and the result saturates at one. This preserves the angular calculation while removing 48 probe arctangents, eight tangent arctangents, and eight final sines per cell. Numerical GPU tests compare against the original angular expression across positive, negative, and opposing steep slopes. The height probes, penumbra law, cache, and number of passes are unchanged; software GPU correctness checks do not establish a device frame-rate gain.

The cache is invalidated by a monotonic solver revision and a key derived from the daylight direction, never by ping-pong buffer index alone: two substeps return to the same buffer while changing its contents. Reset also advances the revision. Camera and viewport changes do not invalidate bed-space lighting. Simulation steps currently advance revisions even when a bed is settled; avoiding those dispatches would require a separate activity reduction.

The time-of-day control interpolates the authored lighting presets, updating the horizon cache as the sun or moon moves. Afternoon retains the original direction with azimuth `-0.8` radians. `BedLighting` accepts its light-change key explicitly so cache behavior and directional response can also be tested independently of the renderer.

At 512 squared the cache occupies 4 MiB. Each refresh uses 62 bilinear height probes per cell; the old fragment path used 18 per screen pixel. Surface fragments now fetch four cached lighting values instead. This bounds expensive lighting work independently of DPR, but can cost more at small viewports or low simulation-to-screen resolution ratios. Frame-time gains have not been benchmarked; GPU tests validate planes, trenches, cache invalidation, shader execution, and offscreen image output. Visual acceptance remains manual.

Steep faces also receive a ground-bounce term in the surface shader: sun reflected off the surrounding sand, scaled by how much ground a face sees (`0.5 · (1 − n.y)` of the geometric normal), times the sand albedo, the sunlit ground's cosine, and sky occlusion. Flat sand and shallow grooves are essentially unchanged. Without it, the shadow sides of poured cones, drip castles and footprints fell to near-black, which real beach shadows never do because the bright sand around them lights them from below.

Where the per-cell sun visibility is a partial shadow (between 0.02 and 0.98), the surface traces the same 14 samples and penumbra law from the exact pixel position and height. Bilinear interpolation of a per-cell visibility drew grid-stepped shadow edges behind tall shapes, especially under a low sun; the trace runs only on those boundary pixels, so its cost stays a small fraction of the frame.
