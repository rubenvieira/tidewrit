# Airborne sand

Fast drawing uses the existing mass-conserving bed/particle handoff rather than a decorative spray emitter. The dense heightfield remains authoritative for normal drawing. Once contact crosses the fast-impact threshold, the advancing contact cap allocates an ejection budget from material that is actually being mobilized there; inactive ballistic grains atomically claim that budget and the same claimed amount is subtracted from the bed through the fixed-point exchange buffer.

Impact parcels use a smaller mass range than the older loose-bed agitation grains so the same conserved airborne mass resolves into a visibly dense spray. A claim may take the remaining fraction of a cell budget instead of requiring one full nominal parcel, which prevents small frontier budgets from disappearing at the end of a substep. Unclaimed budget is never removed from the bed.

Launch energy is tied to the current contact velocity and the local advancing face. Fast impact parcels inherit a substantial fraction of the finger/front horizontal momentum. Vertical launch uses a biased distribution: many low and medium trajectories, a smaller energetic tail, and additional lift when the contact is driving uphill into a sand bank. At a representative 1.1 m/s flat-bed stroke the intended distribution is centimeter-scale, not millimeter-scale; most grains remain short-lived while rare grains rise several centimeters. Quadratic-like speed damping provides bounded aerodynamic drag before gravity and bed collision are applied. The sign of the particle lifetime lane marks fast-impact parcels so this new drag does not alter the older loose-bed agitation trajectory used by ordinary drawing. Landed parcel mass still returns to the heightfield.

Airborne rendering preserves the physical grain radius but gives subpixel grains a minimum raster footprint with area compensation, so a grain does not disappear only because its center falls between framebuffer samples. The footprint is stretched over a short camera-space exposure interval according to the grain velocity, producing bounded motion streaks without changing the simulated trajectory. Fragment depth uses the physical sphere radius even when the optical footprint is enlarged: enlarging a filtered point must not move its surface toward the camera or let a hidden particle protrude through the bed. A GPU regression runs the shipped particle fragment at several optical radii and checks that physical depth remains identical. Grains use premultiplied alpha blending and do not write depth; the opaque bed depth still rejects hidden grains.

Trail placement uses the same capped exposure length as its geometry. The leading end therefore remains at the current simulated particle center even when the 3.5 ms exposure would exceed the six-pixel trail limit. Previously only the trail length was capped, while its center kept moving backward with speed, making fast grains appear detached from their trajectories. Stationary and uncapped trails retain their existing footprint.

The separate solar-glint attachment follows the actual direct-light energy and the same palm-shadow visibility gate already used by the particle's colored sparkle. Its reference luminance comes from the authored afternoon preset, keeping the unshadowed afternoon gain unchanged; night light produces correspondingly weaker flashes, and zero direct light produces none. Kinetic particles use the bed material's 8% solar-glint gain to respect their matte polymer coating. These changes reuse the existing light uniform and shadow sample, leaving grain color, ambient response, exposure, and post-processing intact. An actual-fragment GPU fixture verifies daylight gain, dim and absent light, partial/full palm shade, and kinetic attenuation independently of the facet direction.

Airborne grains also cast a collective shadow. A fixed 512-square world-space optical-depth pass projects active grains along the authored sun direction onto the sand plane. Each physical shadow is filtered to at least roughly one shadow texel and its density is area-compensated; overlapping grains add optical depth. The bed converts the accumulated value to direct-light transmittance. This intentionally produces a soft population shadow rather than conspicuous dark circles under individual submillimeter grains.

Penumbra growth redistributes a fixed optical-depth budget instead of increasing it with altitude. That budget uses the physical grain area and preserves the existing ground-level gain and softness cap: `min(7 · physicalRadius², 0.26 · baseRadius²)`, where `baseRadius` is the larger of the physical radius and the minimum filtered footprint. Dividing by the expanded optical radius squared conserves this budget as the shadow softens. Freezing the cap into the ground-level budget also prevents larger parcels from regaining capped energy at higher altitudes. GPU tests average 64 subtexel phases for both ordinary and larger capped parcels; they allow for RGBA8 quantization while checking stable integrated energy and increasing penumbra width.

The shadow pass adds one bounded instanced draw of the existing particle pool per rendered frame and one 512-square RGBA8 texture. It does not add a simulation dispatch, CPU particle readback, sorting, temporal accumulation, or a lower-quality platform fallback.

Grain contact now sweeps the flight segment through the heightfield's grid cells, splitting each crossed cell at its mesh diagonal and intersecting the resulting planes analytically. A narrow ridge can therefore stop a grain even when both flight endpoints are clear of it; a grazing collision does not depend on a fixed set of probe positions. The walk visits at most sixteen cells and exits at the first hit. Horizontal flight is capped to thirteen grid-cell lengths in the Manhattan metric per substep so even pathological input velocities fit that bound. Typical trajectories are unchanged; stationary horizontal axes require only the local triangle. This adds work only to active grains, with no extra dispatch, buffer, readback, or mesh search.

Dry impacts retain the existing restitution and sliding friction. Moisture between 0.08 and 0.6 progressively lowers restitution from 0.22 to 0.035 and increases tangential energy loss, so damp sand catches spray rather than bouncing it like a dry tray. Collisions place the grain just above the first-hit surface; settled mass still enters the same fixed-point ledger. GPU regressions cover both travel directions, near-grazing ridge contact, diagonal peak collisions late in a near-budget walk, unobstructed grid crossings, wet/dry energy loss, and eventual mass return.

Settled parcels return to the three vertices of their contact triangle using barycentric weights, rather than snapping all their mass to one cell. This keeps redeposition continuous across mesh diagonals and cell boundaries and preserves the landing position's center of mass to the ledger's integer precision. Two shares are rounded down and the third receives the exact remaining fixed-point units, so even a one-unit parcel returns all of its mass. Boundary coordinates and vertices are clamped consistently with the bed geometry. Only nonzero shares issue atomics, at most three per landing; there is no added pass, buffer, neighborhood search, or work for grains that remain airborne. GPU regressions exercise both triangle orientations, edge vertices, tiny parcels, integer conservation, and continuity across triangle and cell boundaries.

The tray boundary absorbs only outward horizontal velocity when a grain reaches it. Vertical flight and motion along the edge continue, and inward motion is untouched, including rebounds from a sloping bed. Position clamping alone left particles pinned to the edge while retaining outward energy; the inelastic boundary response now lets them settle without that hidden motion. It uses the existing bounds and no extra collision walk, read, or dispatch. GPU tests cover all four edges, all corners, inward departure and rebound, and eventual mass return. Flight still uses the existing straight segment to the clamped endpoint rather than a separate wall-hit-and-slide trajectory.
